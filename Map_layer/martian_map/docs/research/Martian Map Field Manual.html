    CHAPTER 01

    The challenge, decoded

    If you misread the brief, nothing else matters. Judges score Relevance directly: how well the project answers this exact challenge.

    What the official text asks for

    The first astronauts on Mars will want the best map possible: route and destination details, updates on current conditions, and the data needed to finish the mission quickly and safely. Your job is to build a layered, integrated view of one location or route on the Martian surface that combines data from several NASA science missions and helps a human explorer plan and carry out a Marswalk while doing science along the way.

    Read on the official page, 13 Nov: what it adds

    • Why this challenge exists: Mars Trek and the USGS geologic maps are built for regional and global views, not the hyper-local areas a single spacewalk targets. Your map should zoom to EVA scale.
    • NASA's own example: boxwork formations on Mount Sharp, which Curiosity studied in 2025. That's Gale crater, where our AI4Mars ground truth comes from. Use boxwork as the science goal and Mount Sharp as the location.
    • "At least three kinds of data": the examples given are optical, thermal, spectral, topographic, geophysical, atmospheric, radiation and chemical.
    • Safety first: radiation on Mars differs from Earth, Earth orbit and the Moon, so the brief suggests space weather data. Spacesuits and carried equipment limit mobility.
    • A route map that avoids significant obstacles such as large rock formations. Our model's "big rock" class answers this directly.
    • Point-of-view images from surface missions, so the astronaut knows what to expect on arrival. Our Navcam photos with terrain overlay do exactly this.
    • Formats they suggest: a display in the habitat, a tablet carried outside, or a HUD on the helmet visor.
    • Time frame: past data, predicted future conditions, or none (for stable layers like topography and geology).
    • Mission design: rovers carrying crew and tools, or astronauts working in pairs. Our "on foot vs pressurized rover" toggle fits.
    • Inspiration: look at how NASA maps other worlds (the Moon, Earth, other planets).

    Check yourself

    Why pick Gale crater over a random spot?

    Because Curiosity drove there and took thousands of Navcam photos. That gives you ground truth to test your orbital layers, which is the core of a "valid science" story. A random spot has only orbital data.

    Can you submit a 2-minute video as the demo?

    No. The demo video is up to 30 seconds. Your local event may hold a longer live pitch, so ask your Local Lead.

    CHAPTER 02

    Mars 101 for mapmakers

    Every layer you draw is a physical quantity. Knowing the planet tells you which numbers are plausible and which are bugs.

    QuantityValueWhy your map cares
    Surface gravity3.71 m/s² (0.38 g)Walking feels lighter, but the suit is heavy. Walking-speed models from Earth are a starting point, not the truth.
    Length of a day (sol)24 h 39 min 35 sYour clock must use local solar time on Mars, not Earth time.
    Year687 Earth days ≈ 669 solsSeasons last about twice as long as on Earth.
    Season markerLs 0°–360°Solar longitude. Ls 0 = northern spring. Dust storms are most common around Ls 180–360 (southern spring/summer).
    Surface pressure~600 Pa (≈0.6% of Earth)Thin air: weak wind force, but dust stays in the air for weeks. Ice sublimates instead of melting.
    Atmosphere~95% CO₂Spacesuit everywhere; every EVA is limited by consumables.
    Temperatureabout −125 °C to +20 °CLarge day/night swings. Rock stays warmer at night than sand (useful, see ch. 05).
    Signal delay Earth↔Mars4 to 22 min one wayNo real-time help from Earth. The crew's map must work on its own.
    Surface radiation~0.6–0.7 mSv per day (Curiosity RAD, 2012–13)Roughly a third of the 1.8 mSv/day measured during the cruise to Mars. Longer EVAs add dose.
    Reference radius3,396,190 m (sphere)Used by maps and 3D globes. Use the same value everywhere or distances drift.

    Coordinates on Mars

    NASA and USGS Mars products use planetocentric latitude (angle measured from the planet's center) and east-positive longitude, usually from 0° to 360°. Many web map libraries expect −180° to +180°. Gale crater sits near 5.4°S, 137.8°E, so it is the same place in both systems. Jezero at 18.4°N, 77.5°E also stays the same. A site at 300°E becomes −60°.

    Terrain types you'll talk about

    • Bedrock: solid rock outcrop. Firm footing, but can be sharp. Sharp rocks damaged Curiosity's wheels.
    • Soil (regolith): loose broken rock and dust on top. Usually fine to drive and walk on.
    • Sand: wind-blown grains forming ripples and dunes. Wheels sink and slip. Spirit got stuck in soft sand in 2009 and never got out.
    • Big rocks / boulders: obstacles you must steer around. In AI4Mars, "big rock" means taller than 30 cm and at least 50 cm wide.
    • Dust: very fine particles covering everything. It hides what's under it, which is why surface type is hard to read from orbit.
    CHAPTER 03

    Think like an astronaut

    Your user isn't a scientist at a desk. It's someone in a pressurized suit, thick gloves, limited oxygen, a 20-minute delay to Earth, and nobody coming to rescue them. Design every feature for that person.

    A Marswalk, minute by minute (scenario)

    Sol 34 · night before
    Commander Amira plans tomorrow's walk to a layered rock outcrop 1.8 km away. Questions: is it reachable, how long will it take, what's on the way, and is anything dangerous?
    07:30 local solar time
    Briefing: weather is calm, no dust storm forecast, the route avoids a sand-filled hollow, and the walk-back limit is respected at every point.
    09:00 · EVA starts
    She follows the route. The map shows her position, the next waypoint and time remaining before she must turn back.
    10:10 · science stop
    The map flags a mineral signature in orbital data. She samples, photographs and tags it.
    11:20 · surprise
    Ripples ahead look softer than expected. She asks the map: what's the detour cost? It recalculates.
    13:30 · back at the habitat
    Her photos are added to the map, so tomorrow's crew sees updated ground truth.

    The walk-back rule

    On planetary surfaces, the standard approach is to limit how far crew may be from a safe haven, so they can walk back if the rover breaks down. For Apollo, this distance was calculated from oxygen, cooling water, battery use and walking speed, then updated during the EVA from actual usage. NASA tested whether a 10 km suited walk-back was feasible.

    Design for gloves

    A pressurized glove can't tap tiny buttons. Add a "Suit mode": huge buttons, high contrast, the three key numbers only (time to turn back, distance to next stop, hazard ahead). Judges notice small human details like this.

    CHAPTER 04

    What NASA actually needs

    Build for a real NASA problem and Influence and Validity follow. Here are needs NASA has publicly worked on.

    1. Knowing what the ground is made of

    JPL built SPOC (Soil Property and Object Classification), a machine-learning tool that classifies terrain from images to help rover drivers and onboard software drive safely. It was trained partly with AI4Mars, where volunteers drew boundaries around terrain in Curiosity images and gave each area one of four labels. Rover planners spend four to five hours planning Curiosity's routes, and SPOC saves them time. Your AI layer is a small, open version of a tool NASA really uses. Say exactly that in the pitch.

    2. Sand is the silent killer of missions

    In August 2014, Curiosity entered "Hidden Valley", found the sand ripples too slippery on sol 709, and backed out on sol 710. JPL's project manager said the team needed a better understanding of how the wheels interact with Martian sand ripples. A 2021 study found the Hidden Valley ripples were only about one sixth the height of another sand feature Curiosity had crossed, yet they were a serious hazard. Height and slope did not predict the danger. Material did.

    3. Water, the resource that picks landing sites

    Almost every human mission plan relies on Martian water ice for fuel and other uses. NASA's SWIM project (Subsurface Water Ice Mapping) combines many orbital datasets to rate how likely buried ice is. The ideal zone is cold enough for shallow ice but still sunny enough for solar power. A "resource" layer from SWIM makes your map feel mission-ready.

    4. Exploration Zones

    In 2015 NASA held a workshop on candidate human landing sites and "Exploration Zones": a landing site, a habitat site and regions of interest within reach. One rival team already uses these. Mention them, but don't build your whole project around them.

    5. Working without Earth

    With a 4–22 minute one-way delay, crews must make decisions locally. A map that explains its decision ("NO-GO because the route crosses 400 m of likely sand and the return would exceed the 4 h limit") is more useful than one that only shows colors.

    Fastest path to a working map

    Load the Mars Trek CTX mosaic and MOLA hillshade as WMTS layers. Read the tile URL template from the WMTSCapabilities.xml file. These endpoints work in the browser with no login.

    The physics trick: thermal inertia

    Thermal inertia is how slowly a surface heats up and cools down. Solid rock stores heat, so at night it is still relatively warm. Loose sand and dust lose heat fast and get cold. In a THEMIS night-time infrared image, bright (warm) means rock and dark (cold) means fines. That gives you a physics-based sand indicator from orbit, independent of the AI.

    The six classic Mars-data bugs

    1. Longitude 0–360 mixed with −180..180: your layer appears on the wrong side of the planet.
    2. Planetographic vs planetocentric latitude: small shifts away from the equator.
    3. NoData treated as a real number: a −3.4e38 m "pit" ruins slope and colors.
    4. Pixel size in degrees treated as meters: slopes off by a factor of ~59,000.
    5. Resampling class maps with bilinear: invents classes. Use nearest-neighbour.
    6. Different radius in 2D and 3D: distances disagree between views.
    CHAPTER 07

    Read, clean, use the data

    This is the step-by-step recipe you'll follow for every dataset. The pattern is always the same.

    Step 1 · Download smart

    • Choose your area of interest (AOI) first, as a bounding box, for example around Hidden Valley in Gale. Only download what covers it.
    • Prefer map-projected products (already georeferenced). Raw camera products need extra geometry work you don't have time for.
    • Save the source URL of every file in a DATA_SOURCES.md. You'll need it for References.

    Step 2 · Inspect before you trust

    import rasterio
src = rasterio.open("gale_dtm.tif")
print(src.crs)          # which planet / projection?
print(src.res)          # pixel size: meters or degrees?
print(src.bounds)       # does it cover my AOI?
print(src.nodata)       # the "no measurement" value
print(src.dtypes, src.count)  # data type, number of bands
arr = src.read(1, masked=True)  # masked array hides NoData
print(arr.min(), arr.max())     # plausible? Gale floor is roughly −4,500 m

    For a vector file:

    import geopandas as gpd
dunes = gpd.read_file("dunes.shp")
print(dunes.crs, len(dunes), dunes.columns.tolist())
dunes.plot()   # always look at it!

    Step 3 · Clean

    • Mask NoData: use masked=True, or np.where(a == nodata, np.nan, a).
    • Fix longitudes: convert to one convention for the whole project.
    • Clip to the AOI early: smaller arrays, faster everything.
    • Drop broken rows: weather tables have gaps and sentinel values. Plot them before you use them.
    • Units: elevation in m, temperature in K or °C, pressure in Pa. Write the unit into variable names: temp_c, elev_m.
    from rasterio.mask import mask
from shapely.geometry import box
aoi = box(137.35, -4.75, 137.45, -4.65)   # lon/lat bounding box (example only)
clipped, transform = mask(src, [aoi], crop=True)

    Step 4 · Align (put everything on one grid)

    To combine layers pixel by pixel (slope + sand + thermal), they must share the same CRS, pixel size and extent. Pick your reference grid (usually the DTM) and reproject the others onto it.

    from rasterio.warp import reproject, Resampling
import numpy as np
dst = np.empty((ref.height, ref.width), dtype="float32")
reproject(source=rasterio.band(thermal_src, 1), destination=dst,
          dst_transform=ref.transform, dst_crs=ref.crs,
          resampling=Resampling.bilinear)   # continuous data → bilinear
# categorical (AI classes, masks) → Resampling.nearest

    Turn vector polygons into a raster on the same grid:

    from rasterio.features import rasterize
dunes = dunes.to_crs(ref.crs)
dune_mask = rasterize([(g, 1) for g in dunes.geometry],
                      out_shape=(ref.height, ref.width),
                      transform=ref.transform, fill=0, dtype="uint8")

    Step 5 · Derive (make new layers)

    # slope in degrees from a DEM in meters
elev = ref.read(1, masked=True).filled(np.nan)
px = ref.res[0]                               # meters per pixel
dzdy, dzdx = np.gradient(elev, px)
slope_deg = np.degrees(np.arctan(np.hypot(dzdx, dzdy)))

# combine layers into one hazard score (weights are YOUR stated assumption)
sand_prob = 0.6*ai_sand + 0.25*dune_mask + 0.15*cold_at_night
hazard = np.clip(slope_deg/20, 0, 1)*0.5 + sand_prob*0.5

    Step 6 · Validate (the step that wins science awards)

    • Compare your orbital sand estimate with the AI ground truth at each rover stop: build a confusion matrix and report agreement.
    • Sanity checks: the slope around a known flat landing site should be low, and Hidden Valley should light up as sand.
    • Write down what you couldn't validate. That's honesty, and it scores.

    Step 7 · Export light, serve fast

    • Rasters → PNG overlays with known bounds (simplest), or XYZ tiles with gdal2tiles.py, or COGs.
    • Vectors → GeoJSON, simplified (gdf.simplify(tolerance)), rounded to 5 decimals.
    • The routing grid → a small binary file or JSON of the cost grid, so the browser can run the planner itself.
    • The AI results → precomputed JSON per rover stop: {sol, lon, lat, sand_pct, rock_pct, image_overlay_url}. Don't run the model live in the demo.

    Tabular data: weather and radiation

    import pandas as pd

    CHAPTER 08

    The AI terrain layer

    This is your differentiator. Get it working early, keep it honest, and make the result easy to see on the map.

    Two-scale architecture

    A. Train the segmentation model

    Use the setup from your AI4Mars project sheet: DeepLabV3+ with an ImageNet ResNet-50 encoder, weighted cross-entropy, ignore_index=255, rover and range masks applied, flips and brightness augmentation. Evaluate per-class IoU against the all-soil baseline. On a free Colab GPU, 3–5 epochs at 512 px is enough for a demo-quality model. Save the weights and the numbers.

    B. Put predictions on the map (geolocation)

    1. Each Navcam image belongs to a sol. The PDS label records it, and MSL raw image filenames encode the spacecraft clock, which converts to a sol.
    2. Export Curiosity's waypoints (one point per sol) as GeoJSON from NASA's "Where is the rover" map (MMGIS).
    3. Join image → sol → waypoint. Now every prediction has a lon/lat.
    4. For each stop, compute sand_pct, rock_pct, bedrock_pct over labeled-range pixels (closer than 30 m).
    5. Draw a "ground-truth strip" along the traverse: dots colored by sand percentage. Click one to see the photo with the colored overlay.

    Honest limits to state

    • A waypoint marks where the rover ended the day. The camera saw up to ~30 m around it, in one direction. Treat it as "near here", not an exact pixel.
    • Ground truth exists only along rover tracks. Everywhere else the map relies on orbital evidence, so show lower confidence there.
    • Wheels and boots differ: sand that stops a rover may only slow a walker.

    C. Validate orbit against ground

    # for each rover stop: does the orbital layer say "sandy" where the AI saw sand?
orbital = sample_raster(sand_prob, stops.lon, stops.lat) > 0.5
ground  = stops.sand_pct > 0.30          # threshold = stated assumption
agree = (orbital == ground).mean()
print(f"orbital/ground agreement: {agree:.0%} on {len(stops)} stops")

    Even a modest agreement is a great slide if you explain it. "Orbit catches big dune fields but misses small ripple patches like Hidden Valley. That's exactly why crews need ground-level AI."

    D. Optional stretch: an orbital dune classifier

    JPL's labeled HiRISE dataset has 10,815 landmarks from 232 HiRISE images, including "bright dune" and "dark dune" classes. Fine-tune a small CNN (ResNet-18) and slide it over a HiRISE browse image of your AOI to produce a dune-probability heatmap. Only do this if A–C are done by Saturday evening.

    E. The Hidden Valley replay

    • Get Navcam images from about sols 703–712 (Hidden Valley approach and retreat) from the PDS imaging archive.
    • Run the model and show the sand percentage spiking on the valley floor.
    • Show two routes on the map: slope-only goes in, terrain-aware goes around.
    • Check honestly whether those images are in AI4Mars' training set. If they are, say so, or hold them out of training.
    CHAPTER 09

    Route planning, with a live demo

    A route planner turns layers into a decision. It's also the clearest way to show judges that the AI matters.

    How it works

    1. Turn the map into a grid graph: each pixel is a node, connected to its 8 neighbours.
    2. Give each step a cost (usually time). Forbid steps that break a hard rule (slope too steep).
    3. Run Dijkstra or A* to find the cheapest path. A* is Dijkstra plus a hint ("straight-line distance to goal") so it searches less.

    The cost function

    Tobler's hiking function estimates walking speed from slope: speed (km/h) = 6 · exp(−3.5 · |tan θ + 0.05|). Flat ground gives about 5 km/h, and steep ground slows you down. One rival team multiplies it by a 0.8 "suit factor". All such numbers are assumptions: label them as yours.

    time_step = distance / (tobler(slope) * suit_factor)
if slope > MAX_SLOPE: forbidden
if sand:  time_step *= SAND_PENALTY   # on foot ~1.3, wheeled rover ~4.5 (assumptions)
    Interactive · synthetic terrain for teaching, not real Mars data

    The Hidden Valley lesson, simulated

    A flat, sandy valley sits between start (left) and goal (right). Hills around it are firmer but steeper. Cells steeper than 20° are forbidden. Pick a planner:

    sand (AI layer) darker = higher too steep (>20°) planned route
    –distance
    –time (no sand penalty)
    –meters on sand
    –steepest step

    Notice what happens: the slope-only planner and the walker both cross the valley, but the rover, for which sand is a trap, goes around. Same map, different traveller, different answer. That nuance is a strong Validity point.

    Extras that make the planner feel real

    • Elevation + hazard profile under the map: a chart along the route showing height, slope and sand bands.
    • Turn-back point: the farthest point from which the walk back still fits the remaining time budget.
    • Isochrones: run Dijkstra once from the habitat without a goal, then draw 1 h / 2 h / 4 h contour lines.
    • Explain the route: "Detour adds 3 min and avoids 557 m of likely sand."
    • Run it in a Web Worker so the page doesn't freeze on big grids.

    Phone / tablet performanceSmoothHeavy
    Build time in 48 hHoursA day, plus terrain data prep
    Bug riskLowHigh (terrain tiles, ellipsoid, camera)

    Recommendation

    2D is the planning tool. 3D is the "fly the route" preview for the selected route only, over your AOI's terrain model. If time runs out, 3D gets cut, not 2D. Rival teams already have impressive 3D globes. You won't beat them on 3D, so win on the decision quality.

    CHAPTER 14

    Brainstorming method

    A good hackathon brainstorm takes 60–90 minutes and ends with a cut list. Here's a structure that works.

    1 · Frame (10 min): "How might we…" questions

    • How might we tell an astronaut where the ground is soft before she steps on it?
    • How might we turn rover photos into a map layer?
    • How might we show how confident the map is?
    • How might we explain a NO-GO in one sentence?
    • How might every crew photo improve tomorrow's map?
    • How might the map work with a 20-minute delay to Earth?
    • How might a gloved hand use the map?
    • How might we prove our sand layer is right?

    2 · Persona (5 min)

    Write one card: Commander Amira, geologist, EVA lead. Goal: sample the layered outcrop and return safely. Fears: getting stuck, running out of time, missing the best sample. Context: suit, gloves, 4-hour EVA budget, no GPS, no live help. Pin it where everyone sees it.

    3 · Crazy 8s (8 min)

    Each person folds a sheet into 8 boxes and sketches 8 screen ideas in 8 minutes, one per minute. Speed beats quality here. Then everyone presents in 1 minute.

    4 · Impact / effort (15 min)

    5 · Decide (10 min)

    • Write the one-sentence pitch. If a feature doesn't serve that sentence, it waits.
    • Write the demo script first (chapter 15), then build only what the script shows.
    • Agree on file schemas between data, ML and frontend.
    • Agree on cut times: what gets dropped at hour 30 if behind.

    Questions to ask NASA experts / mentors on the event channels

    • What slope limit would you assume for a suited walker vs a pressurized rover?
    • Is a 30 m "near the rover" radius reasonable for placing Navcam-based labels?
    • Which dataset do you recommend for thermal inertia near Gale?

    Storytelling & pitch

    Presentation is one of five criteria and gets equal weight. Global judges see your project page, your demo link and your repo, not you. Make every artifact tell the same story.

    TimeOn screenVoice / subtitle

    4–8 sSlope map: valley is green and flat"Slope maps said go. The sand said no."

    7-slide deck (if you choose slides)

    1. Title: name, one-liner, team, challenge.
    2. Problem: Hidden Valley in one photo + "slope ≠ safety".
    3. User: Commander Amira and her 4-hour EVA constraints.
    4. Solution: app screenshot with the 4 layers labeled.
    5. How it works: the two-scale architecture sketch, with mission names.
    6. Proof: IoU per class vs baseline, orbit/ground agreement, replay result.
    7. Impact & next: crew reports, Perseverance data, honest limits, links.

    Live local pitch

    Local events usually run live presentations, and some used a "240 Seconds of Glory" format in past years. Ask your Local Lead for this year's time limit. Rules of thumb:

    • Open with the story, not the team intro.
    • Demo from a pre-loaded state (route already set, layers on). Keep a screen recording as backup.
    • Say a number in the first minute: "557 m of sand crossed vs 0 m."
    • End by naming your limits and next steps. Judges trust teams that know where their work stops.
    • Prepare answers: "Why not just slope?" "How accurate is the AI?" "How does this help a walker, not a rover?" "What's new vs existing tools like SPOC?"

    Project page: what to write

    • High-level summary: problem, solution, why it matters, in about 100 words.
    • Project details: how it works, features, tech stack, AI method, validation results, limits.
    • Space agency data: every NASA dataset by name, with the mission and what you used it for.
    • References: every library, model, dataset, image and tool. Tell judges which parts are yours.
    • Screenshots that make sense with no caption: map with sand overlay plus route plus GO card.

    Name ideas

    Firm Ground · SandSense · Marswalk Navigator · Groundtruth Mars · Regolith · Safe Steps. Pick one that is short, pronounceable, and not already used by a rival.
