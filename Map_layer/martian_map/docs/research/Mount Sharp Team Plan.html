F · The geology layer

Why you draw your own map, and what's best

What a geologic map is

A geologic map splits the ground into units: areas of rock or sediment with the same origin and properties. Lines called contacts mark where one unit meets the next. For an astronaut it answers three questions: what am I standing on, how firm is it likely to be, and where is the science?

Why the official maps don't help at walking scale

Your challenge links the USGS-NASA Planetary Geologic Mapping Program. I queried its map database for your boxwork point (137.38°E, 4.80°S). These are all the maps that cover it:

What the scale means

Scale 1:20,000,000 means 1 mm on the map is 20 km on Mars. Your whole box (2.4 × 3 km) would be about 0.1 mm on that map, thinner than a pen line. Even the 1:5,000,000 maps shrink it to about half a millimetre. Using them for a Marswalk is like planning a walk across a park with a world atlas.

A walk needs about 1:5,000, where 1 mm is 5 m. At that scale a single boxwork ridge (up to about 5 m across) shows up. The official 1:60K map of Mount Sharp would be a big step closer, but it isn't published yet.

What's best

Draw your own small geology map of the box (about 1:5,000), anchor every unit to a published source, and show the official USGS map as the zoomed-out context. In the pitch: "The official 1:60K map of Mount Sharp is in progress at USGS; ours is the walking-scale layer that would sit inside it." Ask a mentor whether any preliminary version of the Thomson map can be shared.

What "draw your own" means

In QGIS, put the 25 cm HiRISE image and the 1 m elevation model on screen. Trace the outline of each unit as a polygon, like tracing paper on a photo. Five units, about 6–8 hours. Each polygon stores its source and how sure you are. The brief says existing tools are built for regional views, not single spacewalks. This layer is your direct answer to that line.

Step by step

  1. Download SIM 3292. Clip about 1° around Gale for the zoomed-out inset.
  2. Clip the HiRISE image and DTM to the box (section M). Make a hillshade and the ridge layer (code below).
  3. In QGIS, create geology.gpkg with units (polygons) and contacts (lines). Fields: unit_code, unit_name, source, hazard, science, confidence.
  4. Turn on snapping and "avoid overlap" so neighbouring polygons share edges exactly.
  5. Digitize at about 1:5,000, with a minimum unit of about 25 m. Don't trace every ridge by hand. The ridge layer does that.
  6. Run the contact through the sol 4534 point. Mark uncertain contacts as approximate.
  7. Fix Geometries, then run the Topology Checker: no gaps and no overlaps.
  8. Export geology.geojson for the map. Rasterize unit_code onto the routing grid with nearest neighbour.

G · 3D & 2D on the tablet

3D by default, 2D one tap away, same colours in both

How the switch works

  • 3D (default): Plotly surface of the box from the DTM, coloured in the four colours, with the route on top. Follow your 3D guide: at most 300 × 300 points, height exaggerated ×3 and labelled on screen, built in Python, loaded as JSON.
  • 2D: Leaflet map with the layer panel. Tapping start and goal is more reliable on a flat map, so route editing happens in 2D. The 3D view shows the result.
  • Both views read the same grid.json, route and stops. Switching changes the picture, never the data.
  • 2D layers, bottom to top: basemap · hillshade · geology units · four-colour grid · stops · route · labels.

H · The four colours

One rule decides every colour: hazard × certainty

The colours match your 3D guide (green confirmed safe, orange orbit only, red danger, grey no data) and extend it with live readings.

ColourMeansRuleExamples
✓GreenGO, high certaintyNo hazard found and the evidence is direct: a live scout or suit reading, or rover ground truth within about 30 mA cell beside a Curiosity stop where the AI found bedrock and no sand; a cell the scout just checked
!OrangePossible with extreme care, lower certaintyNo hard limit broken, but either a moderate hazard or evidence that is only orbital or oldSlope 15–20°; sand on foot; a sediment-filled hollow; anywhere known only from orbit
✕RedNO-GOAny hard limit broken, by any evidenceSlope over the limit; big rock; sand for a wheeled rover; soft ground measured by the scout; oxygen or dose limit exceeded
?GreyUnknown, not yet exploredNo usable dataDTM gap; no ground truth and the orbital data is unclear. Send the scout here first.

Certainty ladder

  1. Live: scout or suit sensor, minutes old
  2. Rover ground truth: your AI on Curiosity photos, years old but taken on the ground
  3. Orbital: HiRISE, THEMIS, the DTM
  4. None: grey

When sources disagree, the newest, closest and most direct reading wins. Live readings expire after a time you choose (an assumption), and the cell then falls back to the next level down. A route takes the worst colour of the cells it crosses.

Bright light, a tinted visor or colour-vision deficiency can make red and green hard to tell apart. Every colour therefore also carries an icon (✓ ! ✕ ?). Give grey and orange cells hatching on the map.

// Deterministic rule. The assistant explains it and never overrides it.
// Every number here is an assumption: list it in ASSUMPTIONS.md.
const LIMIT = { slopeNoGo: 20, slopeCaution: 15 };

function cellColour(c, traveller) {        // traveller: "foot" | "rover"
  if (c.evidence === "none") return "grey";
  const noGo = c.slopeDeg > LIMIT.slopeNoGo || c.bigRock ||
               (traveller === "rover" && (c.sand || c.softMeasured));
  if (noGo) return "red";
  const caution = c.slopeDeg > LIMIT.slopeCaution || c.sand ||
                  c.unit === "BXh" || c.evidence === "orbital";
  return caution ? "orange" : "green";     // green needs live or rover evidence
}

function goNoGo(route, suit) {             // route-level checks
  const why = [];
  if (route.colours.includes("red")) why.push("route crosses a NO-GO cell");
  if (suit.o2Min < route.returnMin + suit.reserveMin)
    why.push(`oxygen ${suit.o2Min} min < return ${route.returnMin} + reserve ${suit.reserveMin}`);
  if (route.doseMSv > suit.doseLimitMSv) why.push("dose above limit");
  return { status: why.length ? "NO-GO" : "GO", why };
}

J · Robotic scout & live data

Measure the ground ahead, and trust fresh readings over old maps

NASA precedent

NASA's Ingenuity helicopter was designed for up to five test flights. It flew 72 times and served "as an aerial scout for Perseverance scientists and rover drivers" (JPL). Its mission ended on 25 Jan 2024. On Flight 72, sand ripples that its navigation system struggled to track led to a rough landing that damaged its rotor blades.

Lesson for your concept: a drone over featureless sand has its own hazard. Give it firm landing spots.

The brief itself asks whether your mission "might use rovers or other robotic systems".

K · The assistant

It warns and answers, on screen and by voice, without making things up

It helps the astronaut think. It does not decide. NASA's brief stresses that astronauts bring critical thinking that robots can't.

Five design rules that stop it from making things up

  1. It never decides. Colours and GO / NO-GO come from the rule in section H. The assistant explains the result.
  2. It answers only from app data, through tools: where_am_i(), cell_info(lat, lon), route_summary(), sensor_readings(since), assumptions(), source(dataset). If a tool returns nothing, it says "I don't know. That area is grey," and suggests the scout.
  3. Every answer names its source and the age of the data, for example "REMS, sol 4709" or "scout, 3 min ago".
  4. Rules trigger warnings; the model only words them. A warning still fires, as text and a sound, if the assistant is down.
  5. Test it before the freeze. P5 writes 25 astronaut questions, 5 of which have no answer in the app. It passes when it answers 20 correctly with sources and says "I don't know" to the 5.

How to build it this weekend

  • On Mars vs in the demo: with a 4–22 minute one-way delay to Earth, the real system must run on a computer in the habitat or rover. In the demo it can call a cloud model through a small server function (Vercel or Netlify), so the API key is never in the browser.
  • Model: any LLM with tool calling works. If you use the Claude API, the current default model is claude-opus-5-5, with tool use. Decide in O3.
  • Voice: the browser's text-to-speech reads warnings and answers aloud. Browser speech recognition support varies, so test it on your tablet first and keep a push-to-talk text box as a fallback.
  • Helmet: voice plus one subtitle line. Tablet: a chat panel that shows the sources.
  • Effort: 6–10 person-hours. The tool functions are thin wrappers over files the app already loads.

M · Cleaning the data

Eight rules, then one recipe per dataset

Rules for every file

  1. Keep data/raw/ read-only. Scripts read raw files and write to data/clean/, numbered in run order (01_dtm.py, 02_geology.py…).
  2. Use one web CRS: planetocentric latitude, east longitude from −180 to 180, a sphere with R = 3,396,190 m (+proj=longlat +R=3396190 +no_defs). Do the analysis in the DTM's own metre grid.
  3. Clip to the box before anything else.
  4. Mask NoData before any maths. HiRISE DTMs use −3.4028 × 10³⁸.
  5. Put units in names: elev_m, slope_deg, temp_c, dose_mSv.
  6. Continuous data → bilinear or mean. Classes → nearest or mode. Hazards → maximum.
  7. Print before and after statistics for every step, and look at a picture.
  8. Write each source URL into DATA_SOURCES.md when you download it.
# 01_dtm.py: clip, mask, fill small holes, remove spikes, slope
import numpy as np, rasterio
from rasterio.warp import transform_bounds
from rasterio.windows import from_bounds
from rasterio.fill import fillnodata
from scipy.ndimage import median_filter

MARS_LL = "+proj=longlat +R=3396190 +no_defs"
BOX = (137.36, -4.83, 137.40, -4.78)       # W, S, E, N
src = rasterio.open("data/raw/DTEEC_019698_1750_019988_1750_U01.IMG")
print(src.crs, src.res, src.nodata)        # res must be ~1.0 (metres)

win = from_bounds(*transform_bounds(MARS_LL, src.crs, *BOX),
                  transform=src.transform).round_offsets().round_lengths()
z = src.read(1, window=win, masked=True)
z = np.ma.masked_less(z, -1e30)            # belt and braces for -3.4e38
print(z.min(), z.max(), f"{z.mask.mean():.1%} empty")   # waypoints say ~ -3735 .. -3472 m

filled = fillnodata(z.filled(np.nan).astype("float32"),
                    mask=(~z.mask).astype("uint8"), max_search_distance=10)
interpolated = z.mask & ~np.isnan(filled)  # show these as lower certainty

med = median_filter(np.nan_to_num(filled, nan=float(np.nanmean(filled))), size=5)
spike = np.abs(filled - med) > 5           # metres; a starting threshold, check the result
filled[spike] = med[spike]

dzdy, dzdx = np.gradient(filled, src.res[0])
slope_deg = np.degrees(np.arctan(np.hypot(dzdx, dzdy)))
print(np.nanpercentile(slope_deg, [50, 95, 99]))   # nearly all red? the units are wrong
